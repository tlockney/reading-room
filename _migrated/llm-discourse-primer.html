§ 01Anatomy: what "120B parameters" means

A model's parameters (interchangeably, weights) are the learned numbers—the entries in its matrices. "120B" means 120 billion of them. Parameter count is the standard shorthand for model size and rough capability class, though it's an increasingly leaky proxy.

Why the number matters practically: memory. Each parameter is stored at some precision—16-bit floating point (fp16/bf16) is the common native format, 2 bytes each. So a 120B model at fp16 needs ~240 GB just to hold the weights, before anything else.

One multiplication—parameters × bytes per parameter—explains most conversations about what hardware can run what model.

Model sizefp16 (2 B/param)8-bit4-bit
8B~16 GB~8 GB~4 GB
70B~140 GB~70 GB~35 GB
120B~240 GB~120 GB~60 GB

Dense vs. MoE (Mixture of Experts): in a dense model, every parameter participates in every token. An MoE model contains many "expert" sub-networks and routes each token through only a few—so you'll see phrasing like "120B total, 5B active." Total parameters determine memory; active parameters determine per-token compute. This is why MoE models can be both large and fast, and why comparing models by headline parameter count alone misleads.

The architecture those matrices implement is, in every current frontier model, the transformer—and its core mechanism is attention, which lets each token look back at every earlier token when predicting the next one. That's all the vocabulary you need for now; §6's KV-cache is attention wearing its operational costume, and Phase 4 builds the real picture.

Model families ship in sizes (e.g., 3B/12B/70B variants of the same design), trading capability against cost. Discourse convention: the size suffix in a model name is almost always the parameter count. Two adjacent terms: a frontier model is one at the current capability edge (and a frontier lab is one that trains them); multimodal—or "omni"—means the model consumes and sometimes produces images, audio, or video as well as text, by mapping them into the same token stream.

§ 02Pretraining: where the model comes from

Pretraining is the giant, expensive phase: next-token prediction over trillions of tokens of text. It produces a base model—a raw text-continuation engine with broad knowledge but no inclination to be helpful, follow instructions, or stop talking. When people cite "$100M training runs," this is the phase they mean.

Terms that live here: tokens as the unit of training data ("trained on 15T tokens"), compute measured in FLOPs or GPU-hours, and scaling laws—the empirical finding that capability improves predictably with more parameters, data, and compute, which drove the size race.

Tokens deserve a beat of their own, because they're the unit of everything—training data, context limits, billing, and latency. A tokenizer (BPE—byte-pair encoding—or a descendant) splits text into chunks from a fixed vocabulary of a few hundred thousand entries; common words are one token, rare words several. The working rule: a token is ~4 characters, ~¾ of an English word. Token counts surprise people in predictable places: code, non-English text, and numbers all fragment into more tokens than the character count suggests—which is why the same "page of text" can cost meaningfully different amounts depending on what's on it. When a pricing page says "$X per million input tokens," this is the unit it means.

§ 03Post-training: where the assistant comes from

Post-training is everything done to a base model to make it a usable product. It's cheap relative to pretraining and it's where much of the current action is. The main moves:

  • SFT (supervised fine-tuning): train on curated example conversations—"here's a prompt, here's a good response." Teaches format and instruction-following.
  • Preference optimization—RLHF, DPO: train on comparisons ("response A beats response B") to shape tone, helpfulness, and refusal behavior. RLHF does this with reinforcement learning; DPO gets similar effect with a simpler, more stable method. You don't need the mechanics yet—just that this is the step that makes models behave.
  • RL on verifiable rewards: the newer wave behind "reasoning models"—train the model to produce long chains of thought, rewarding it when the final answer checks out (math, code, tests). When discourse mentions "thinking models," "test-time compute," or "reasoning traces," this is the lineage.

So when a lab says "we improved the model through post-training," they changed its behavior without a new pretraining run. Base model = raw material; post-training = the product.

§ 04Fine-tuning: the term doing double duty

Confusingly, fine-tuning describes both a lab's post-training and what you as a customer can do: continue training a released model on your own data. For the customer sense:

  • Full fine-tuning updates all weights—expensive, rarely justified.
  • LoRA / PEFT (parameter-efficient fine-tuning) trains a small adapter layer while freezing the base—cheap enough to be routine, and what "fine-tuned on our data" almost always means in practice.
Decision intuition

Fine-tuning teaches behavior and form—style, format, domain vocabulary. It is a poor tool for adding knowledge, which is what retrieval (RAG) is for. Phase 4 turns this into a real decision framework; the Embeddings & Retrieval primer carries the fuller comparison.

§ 05Making models smaller: quantization and distillation

Quantization stores weights at lower precision—8-bit, 4-bit, occasionally lower—shrinking memory footprint (that 240 GB model at 4-bit: ~60 GB) and speeding inference, at some quality cost that is often surprisingly small down to 4-bit. This is the enabling technology of the local/on-device model scene. Format names you'll see in the wild—GGUF (the llama.cpp ecosystem's file format), AWQ, GPTQ—are quantization methods and containers; "Q4" in a filename means 4-bit. The Local Models & Hardware guide cashes this section out on real machines.

Distillation trains a small model to imitate a large one's outputs—transferring capability into a cheaper package. Many small released models are distillations of frontier ones, acknowledged or otherwise.

§ 06Inference: what serving a model actually involves

Generation happens in two phases with very different profiles:

  • Prefill: the model processes your whole prompt in one parallel pass. Compute-heavy, but fast per token.
  • Decode: it then generates output one token at a time, each step conditioned on everything before. This sequential loop is why responses stream.

The KV-cache is the trick that makes decode viable: during attention, each token produces key and value vectors that every later token needs to look at. Rather than recomputing them for the entire context on every new token, the model caches them. Consequences worth internalizing:

  • KV-cache memory grows with context length—this, not the weights, is what blows up GPU memory at long context, and it's the real cost of "1M-token context windows."
  • It's why prompt caching exists as a billed API feature: a reused system prompt's KV-cache can be stored and reattached, skipping prefill. Large discounts on cached input tokens are the provider passing that saving along.
  • Much serving-infrastructure discourse (paged attention, batching strategies) is KV-cache memory management.

The context window is the maximum tokens (prompt + output) the model handles per request. Related decode-side vocabulary: temperature/top-p (sampling randomness), speculative decoding (a small model drafts tokens the big one verifies—an inference speedup, not a quality change).

The two phases also explain the two latency numbers serving discourse cares about: TTFT (time to first token) is prefill wearing its observable clothes, and tokens/sec is decode wearing its. A provider quoting great tokens/sec with poor TTFT is fast at streaming and slow to start—different fixes, different costs.

§ 07The application layer

Everything above is the model; most day-to-day discourse happens in the layer built on top of it. The vocabulary:

  • System prompt: standing instructions sent with every request, distinct from the user's message. Where products put their persona, rules, and guardrails—and the thing prompt caching (§6) exists to make cheap.
  • Structured output / function calling: making the model emit machine-parseable JSON matching a schema, rather than prose. The plumbing under every "the model filled in the form" feature.
  • Tool use: the request/execute/return loop—model asks for a tool call, your code runs it, the result goes back in, the model continues. An agent is this loop run repeatedly toward a goal; when discourse says "agentic," it means products built on that loop. Unit 1.2 demystifies it in an afternoon.
  • MCP (Model Context Protocol): an open standard for plugging tools and data sources into models—USB for tool use, so every product doesn't invent its own connector format.
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): fetch relevant material first, put it in the prompt, instruct the model to ground its answer in it. The standard way to give a model knowledge it wasn't trained on—Embeddings & Retrieval owns the mechanics.
  • Context engineering: the successor term to "prompt engineering," reflecting where the craft actually moved—deciding what goes in the context window (instructions, retrieved documents, tool results, history) rather than wordsmithing a single prompt.

§ 08Failure modes and safety

The vocabulary an engineering leader gets asked about most:

  • Hallucination (or confabulation): the model states falsehoods fluently. Not a bug being patched out—a direct consequence of next-token prediction, which optimizes for plausible continuation, not truth. Mitigations are structural: grounding (give it the facts via RAG and instruct it to stick to them), citations, and evals that measure the rate rather than hoping.
  • Alignment: the umbrella term for making model behavior match human intent—§3's post-training machinery is alignment in practice. In discourse the word stretches from "follows instructions politely" to long-horizon safety debates; note which sense a speaker means.
  • Jailbreak vs. prompt injection—routinely confused, importantly different. A jailbreak is a user talking the model out of its own rules ("ignore your instructions and…"). Prompt injection is an attacker hijacking your application through content it processes—a malicious instruction hidden in a webpage, email, or document your system feeds to the model. Jailbreaks embarrass the model vendor; injection compromises your product, and there is no reliable general defense yet—which makes it an architecture concern (what can the model touch?), not a prompt-wording concern. Phase 5 treats it properly.
  • Red-teaming: adversarial testing—paying people (or models) to break the model before users do.
  • System cards / model cards: the vendor's published documentation of a model's capabilities, limits, and safety evaluations. Worth skimming for what the vendor admits—the failure modes listed there are the ones they couldn't fix.

§ 09Reading a model announcement

The skeptic's checklist for release-day discourse:

  • "Open weights" ≠ open source. You get the weights file, usually under a license with conditions; training data and recipe stay private.
  • Benchmarks (MMLU and successors, coding and math suites) are useful signals with a known failure mode: contamination (test data leaking into training) and teaching-to-the-test.
  • Parse the name: family, size, variant ("-instruct" or "-chat" = post-trained; "-base" = raw), sometimes quantization.
  • The interesting questions are usually: dense or MoE (what does it really cost to run), what post-training approach, what license, and what context length at what quality.
