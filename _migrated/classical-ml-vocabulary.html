§ 01The taxonomy: what kind of learning

Three families, distinguished by what the training data looks like. Nearly every method you'll meet in Phase 2—and most of what vendors are selling—sits cleanly in one of them, so the taxonomy is the first thing that makes the field's sprawl navigable.

Supervised learning: the data comes with answers. Each example is a set of inputs plus the correct output—a house and its sale price, a transaction and whether it was fraud. The model learns the mapping. This is most of applied ML, and it splits into regression (predict a number) and classification (predict a category).

Unsupervised learning: data without answers. The model finds structure on its own—groups of similar customers, points that don't look like the others. Clustering and anomaly detection live here. Harder to evaluate, because there's no “correct” to compare against.

Reinforcement learning: no dataset at all—an agent acts, gets rewards, and learns a policy. Mostly outside this path's scope, except that a variant of it reappears in LLM post-training (RLHF, reasoning-model training).

Problem-shape intuition: “predict this column from those columns” is supervised. “Tell me what's interesting in here” is unsupervised. “Learn to play the game” is reinforcement.

§ 02Data vocabulary

Features are the input columns—what the model gets to look at. In predict churn from tenure, plan, and support tickets, those three are features. The label (or target) is the output column—the thing being predicted. Supervised learning is exactly “features in, label out.”

Tabular data is rows and columns, the spreadsheet/SQL shape. Worth naming because it's the territory classical ML still owns; deep learning's wins came first in unstructured data (images, audio, text).

Feature engineering is constructing better features from raw ones—day-of-week from a timestamp, ratios, rolling averages. In classical ML this is where much of the real work and domain knowledge goes; deep learning's pitch was partly “the network learns the features itself.”

Dimensionality is the number of features. High-dimensional data (thousands of features, few examples) breaks geometric intuition and invites overfitting—the “curse of dimensionality” is that everything becomes far apart and sparse.

§ 03The model families

One paragraph each—what it is, and when it wins.

Linear regression

Fit a weighted sum of the features to predict a number: price ≈ w₁·sqft + w₂·age + b. The weights are directly readable—“each square foot adds $w₁, all else equal”—which makes it the interpretability benchmark everything else is compared against. Wins when relationships are roughly linear, when you need to explain the model to a regulator, or as the baseline you must beat to justify anything fancier.

Logistic regression

Despite the name, a classifier—the naming trap is that “regression” here is historical. It's linear regression pushed through a squashing function so the output is a probability between 0 and 1: “73% likely to churn.” Still linear at heart, still interpretable, and a shockingly strong baseline for most classification problems. If a deep model can't beat logistic regression on your tabular problem, use logistic regression.

Decision trees

A learned flowchart: if tenure < 6 months, go left; if support_tickets > 3, predict churn. Trivially interpretable, handles non-linear relationships and mixed feature types without preprocessing—and overfits catastrophically if grown deep, memorizing the training data branch by branch. Almost never used alone; used in bulk (next two entries).

Random forests

Train hundreds of trees, each on a random subset of the data and features, and average their votes. The randomness makes the trees' errors uncorrelated, so averaging cancels them—an ensemble. Nearly tuning-free, hard to overfit badly, and a robust default for tabular problems: the “just try a random forest first” advice is common because it's good.

Gradient boosting

Also many trees, but built sequentially—each new tree trained to correct the errors of the ensemble so far. More knobs than a forest, more prone to overfitting if pushed, and, tuned well, the strongest thing on tabular data. This is XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost—the family that wins tabular Kaggle competitions and quietly powers a large share of production ML.

Why not neural nets here

Tabular data still belongs to boosted trees rather than deep learning: tabular features are already meaningful (no representation to learn), datasets are often small by deep-learning standards, and trees natively handle the sharp thresholds and interactions tabular data is full of.

k-means

The workhorse clusterer: pick k, and the algorithm partitions points into k groups around k centers, alternately assigning points to the nearest center and moving centers to their group's mean. Fast, simple, and its two famous weaknesses are that you must choose k yourself and it assumes clusters are roughly round blobs.

Isolation forests

Anomaly detection by a clever inversion: build random trees that split the data arbitrarily, and measure how quickly each point gets isolated in its own leaf. Outliers isolate in a few splits; normal points, buried in dense regions, take many. No labels needed, which matters because anomalies are precisely what you don't have labeled examples of. The natural tool for “flag the weird meter readings.”

Honorable mentions

kNN (predict from the k nearest examples—no training, all lookup), SVMs (find the maximally separating boundary; pre-boosting champions, now niche), naive Bayes (count-based probabilities under a feature-independence assumption; the classic spam filter), PCA (unsupervised dimensionality reduction—recast the features as fewer, decorrelated ones). You'll meet these in docs and papers more often than you'll reach for them.

§ 04The discipline

The vocabulary of not fooling yourself—the section that makes you dangerous in a review meeting.

Train / validation / test splits

The model learns on the training set. You tune knobs by checking the validation set. The test set is opened once, at the end, to estimate real-world performance. The moment you make a decision based on test-set results, it has silently become a validation set and its number is no longer honest.

Cross-validation

When data is scarce, rotate the split: train on 4/5 of the data, validate on the remaining fifth, repeat five times with a different fifth held out, average. More reliable than a single split, at 5× the training cost.

Overfitting, underfitting, bias–variance

Overfitting: the model memorized the training data's noise and quirks—great training score, poor on anything new. Underfitting: the model is too simple to capture the real pattern—poor everywhere. The diagnostic is the gap: high train score with a much lower validation score means overfit.

Bias–variance is the formal frame for that tradeoff. Bias is error from being too rigid (a linear model on a curved pattern); variance is error from being too flexible (a deep tree that changes wildly with each new sample). Model complexity trades one for the other; the game is finding the sweet spot for your data size.

Leakage

Information about the answer sneaking into the features—the most common way ML projects produce fake results. The sneaky version is temporal: predicting customer churn using a feature like days_since_last_login computed after the churn happened, or normalizing the whole dataset before splitting so the training set has statistically “seen” the test set.

Caveat · The production collapse

Leaky models score brilliantly in development and collapse in production, because production doesn't have the leaked future. When a result looks too good, hunt for leakage before celebrating.

Regularization and hyperparameters

Regularization penalizes complexity during training to curb overfitting—L2/ridge shrinks all weights smoothly; L1/lasso drives some to exactly zero, doubling as feature selection. The knob that turns the bias–variance dial directly; the C or alpha parameter you'll see everywhere in scikit-learn is this.

Parameters the model learns (weights, splits); hyperparameters you choose (tree depth, k, regularization strength). Tuning is a search—grid or random—scored by cross-validation, and the validation set exists precisely so this search doesn't burn the test set.

Baselines

The dumb model you must beat: predict the mean, predict the majority class, predict “same as yesterday.” A 94%-accurate model is impressive until you learn that always guessing “no fraud” scores 99%. Always demand the baseline number next to the model's number; the gap between them is the model's actual contribution.

§ 05The metric zoo

Why accuracy fails. Accuracy—fraction of predictions that were right—is the intuitive metric and the misleading one, because it hides which errors happen. On imbalanced data (fraud at 1%, rare-disease screening) a model that never fires scores 99%. Every metric below exists to see what accuracy hides.

The confusion matrix

The 2×2 table underneath everything: predictions vs. reality. Every classification metric is an arithmetic view of these four cells.

Precision and recall—two different questions

Precision: of the things the model flagged, how many were real? The false-alarm question. Low precision = alert fatigue. Recall: of the real things out there, how many did the model catch? The miss question. Low recall = silent failures.

They trade off against each other through the decision threshold: flag more aggressively and recall rises while precision falls. Which one matters more is a product decision, not an ML one—a leak-detection alert that pages a human wants high precision; a safety screen wants high recall. Asking “precision or recall?” when someone reports a single number is the reviewer move.

The rest of the zoo

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall—a single number when you must have one, at the cost of hiding the tradeoff you probably care about.

ROC/AUC, honestly: a curve tracing the tradeoff across every possible threshold, and the area under it—a threshold-independent summary of ranking quality, where 0.5 is coin-flipping and 1.0 is perfect. Useful for comparing models; routinely flattering on imbalanced data (precision-recall curves are the skeptic's alternative). It measures whether the model ranks positives above negatives, not whether its deployed threshold makes good decisions.

Regression metrics: MAE (mean absolute error)—average miss, in the target's own units, robust to outliers. RMSE (root mean squared error)—similar but squares errors first, so large misses dominate; the one to watch when big errors are disproportionately costly. RMSE ≥ MAE always; a wide gap means the errors include some whoppers.

The base-rate connection

Precision and recall are conditional probabilities, and their behavior under rare events is the base-rate math in Statistics for Engineers—a 99%-accurate detector of a 1-in-1000 event is mostly wrong when it fires. That single idea, transplanted into metric form, is most of what goes wrong in anomaly-detection products.

§ 06Reading an ML claim

The skeptic's checklist, for papers, vendor decks, and your own team's demos alike:

  • What's the baseline? No baseline, no claim. “94% accurate” means nothing without “and always-predict-no scores 99%.”
  • How was it validated? A single train/test split, or cross-validation? Was the test set used once, or repeatedly (and thus burned)? For time-series: was the split chronological? Random splits on temporal data are leakage by construction—the model trains on the future.
  • What's the class balance? Imbalance determines which metrics are honest. Accuracy on a 99/1 problem is a red flag by itself.
  • Where could leakage hide? Features computed after the outcome, preprocessing fit on the full dataset, duplicate rows straddling the split, IDs that encode the answer.
  • Precision or recall—and who chose the threshold? A single headline number hides the tradeoff and the product decision embedded in it.
  • Would plain code do? The question from Phase 5, worth carrying everywhere: many an “ML system” is a threshold and a lookup table wearing a costume.
