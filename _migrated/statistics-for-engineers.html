§ 01Distributions: the shape of your data

You already have more statistical intuition than you think—you've reasoned about p99 latency, flaky tests, and error budgets for years. This primer mostly attaches proper names and sharper edges to instincts you already carry. It starts where all of it starts: a distribution is the answer to "if I looked at every value, what shape would the pile make?" Before any summary number means anything, you need the shape, because the shape determines which summaries lie.

Three shapes cover most of what you'll meet:

  • Normal (the bell curve). Symmetric, concentrated around the middle, tails fading fast. Shows up when a value is the sum of many small independent effects—measurement noise, human heights. The comfortable case: mean and standard deviation describe it fully.
  • Skewed. Most values on one side, a stretched tail on the other. Incomes, file sizes, home prices.
  • Long-tailed. Skew's extreme cousin: most values small, a few enormous—and the enormous ones dominate the totals. Request latency, outage durations, viral posts.

You already know this distinction as percentiles. The reason dashboards show p50 and p99 instead of average latency is exactly that latency is long-tailed: the mean is dragged upward by the tail until it describes no request anyone actually experienced. "Mean latency 80ms" can be true while half of requests finish in 30ms and the worst 1% take 4 seconds. A percentile—"p99 = 1.2s" means 99% of values sit at or below 1.2s—is just a direct reading off the distribution, no shape assumption required.

The operational habit: plot before you summarize. A histogram takes ten seconds and immediately tells you whether the summary numbers you're about to compute are meaningful. Most statistical lies to engineers are a single number quietly standing in for a shape it doesn't resemble.

§ 02Center and spread

Mean is the sum divided by the count. It's the right center for symmetric distributions and the wrong one for skewed data, because every value pulls on it—one outlier can drag it anywhere. Median is the middle value when sorted: half above, half below. Outliers can't budge it. The classic anchor: when a billionaire walks into a bar, the mean wealth of everyone inside jumps by tens of millions; the median barely moves. If someone reports a mean and the data might be skewed, ask for the median too; a large gap between them is the skew, quantified. (Mode—the most common value—rarely earns its keep outside categorical data.)

Spread is the more neglected half. Variance is the average squared distance from the mean; standard deviation (sd) is its square root, which puts it back in the original units—milliseconds, dollars—so you can think with it. The intuition worth keeping, for roughly normal data:

  • ~68% of values fall within 1 sd of the mean
  • ~95% within 2 sd
  • ~99.7% within 3 sd

So "2 sd" is a serviceable everyday meaning for "surprisingly far from typical," and it's the spirit behind control-chart alerting and most anomaly thresholds. For skewed data, sd inherits all of the mean's problems—reach for percentiles instead (the interquartile range, p10–p90, whatever fits the question).

The reflex this section installs: never accept a center without asking for the spread. "Average handling time improved to 40s" is uninterpretable until you know whether values cluster at 38–42 or range from 5 to 300.

§ 03Sampling and uncertainty

You almost never see the population (all requests, all users, all possible eval questions). You see a sample, and you're hoping it speaks for the rest. Everything in this section is about how loudly a sample can lie.

Small samples lie loudly. Flip a fair coin 10 times and getting 7 heads is unremarkable—it happens about 12% of the time. Nobody concludes the coin is biased at 7/10; yet the same evidence, dressed as "70% of the first ten beta users converted," routinely gets extrapolated into a forecast. The instinct to build: for any proportion, mentally attach the sample size. 70% of 10 and 70% of 10,000 are different facts.

The standard error is the standard deviation of the estimate itself—how much the number you computed would wobble if you could rerun the whole sampling process. It shrinks with the square root of sample size, and that √n is the tyranny of the whole field: to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. A hundred times more data buys you one extra decimal place of confidence (√100 = 10). This is why "just collect a bit more" rarely rescues a marginal result.

The central limit theorem (CLT) is the reason any of this machinery works: averages of samples are approximately normally distributed even when the underlying data isn't. Your latency data can be as long-tailed as it likes; the average of repeated samples of it still behaves like a bell curve. That's what licenses the 68/95/99.7 reasoning about estimates, and it's why the normal distribution is everywhere in statistics despite raw data rarely being normal.

A confidence interval packages this into an honest error bar: instead of "80% correct," you report "80%, and the plausible range given this sample size is roughly 55–93%." That's not an exotic calculation—for a proportion near 80% with n=15, the standard error is about 10 percentage points, and a 95% interval is roughly ±2 of those: 20 points each way. (The exact interval is lopsided because a score can't pass 100%, hence 55–93 rather than a naive 60–100. For intuition, the shortcut is plenty.)

Caveat · Sit with this one

A 15-question eval doesn't measure "80%." It measures "somewhere in the 60s to the 90s." Numbers reported without their interval aren't wrong, exactly—they're claiming a precision they don't have.

§ 04Correlation

Correlation (the coefficient r, from −1 to 1) measures how tightly two variables move together linearly. r = 0.9: knowing one tells you a lot about the other. r = 0: no linear relationship—though possibly a strong nonlinear one (a perfect U-shape scores near zero), which is one more reason to plot before you summarize.

The famous trap has a mechanism worth naming, not just a slogan. Ice cream sales correlate with drowning deaths—not because either causes the other, but because summer drives both. A confounder is a third variable that moves the two you're looking at, manufacturing a relationship between them. Correlation ≠ causation isn't skepticism for its own sake; it's the specific question "what else could be moving both of these?" asked before believing any observational chart. (Confounders are everywhere in product data: cohort age, seasonality, and "power users do everything more" account for a remarkable fraction of exciting dashboard findings.)

Two working notes for later phases. First, with enough variable pairs, strong correlations appear by pure chance—mine 1,000 metrics against each other and dazzling spurious correlations are guaranteed. Second, correlated features are why regression coefficients resist casual reading: when two inputs move together, the model can shift credit between them freely, so "the coefficient on X is small" doesn't mean X doesn't matter. That lands in Phase 2's regression unit.

§ 05Is the difference real?

The section this primer exists for. You will constantly compare two numbers from noisy processes—prompt A vs. prompt B, this week vs. last week, vendor claim vs. your test—and the default failure mode is treating any gap as signal.

The worked example, from unit 1.6 of the path: your RAG pipeline scores 12/15 on your eval set. You tweak the prompt; it scores 13/15. Ship the tweak?

Run the reasoning from § 03. If the pipeline's true accuracy were exactly 80% and unchanged, rerunning a 15-question eval produces 12 sometimes, 11 sometimes, 13 or 14 sometimes—the standard error at n=15 is about one and a half questions. A one-question improvement is comfortably inside the band that pure noise produces. The honest reading of 12/15 → 13/15 is: no detectable difference. And the noise stacks: the model's own sampling varies run to run, retrieval can tie-break differently, and if an LLM-as-judge does the grading, the judge is a stochastic process too—the same answer can grade differently on two passes. Every layer widens the band.

What actually helps, in rough order of cost-effectiveness:

  • Pair the comparison. Run both configurations on the same questions and look at the flips. This removes question-difficulty variance—the largest noise source—for free. If B beats A because two questions flipped up and one flipped down, you're looking at a coin-flip pattern, not an improvement. Want a 2-point win to be persuasive at small n? Something like five-up-zero-down is the shape of real signal.
  • Read the flipped items. Fifteen questions is small enough to read. Which answers changed, and is the change the kind your edit should cause? At small n, a mechanism you can articulate is worth more than the arithmetic.
  • Repeat the runs. Run each configuration 3–5 times. If the run-to-run ranges overlap heavily, you don't have a result—you have two samples from the same puddle. The spread you observe is also your first real measurement of the pipeline's own variance, which is worth knowing regardless.
  • Grow the eval set. Noise shrinks with √n: 15 → 100 questions cuts the band by ~2.5×. Detecting a genuine 5-point improvement reliably takes a few hundred items. This is the real argument that the eval set is an asset to invest in, not ceremony to satisfy.

The transferable habit is a review-meeting question: "How many items, how many runs, and what's the run-to-run spread?" If nobody knows the spread, the headline number isn't a measurement yet—it's an anecdote with a decimal point.

This applies verbatim to A/B test readouts, benchmark deltas in vendor decks, and "the new model is better" claims of every kind.

§ 06Base rates: why accurate detectors are usually wrong

Conditional probability—P(A given B)—is the last core idea, and the place engineer intuition most reliably faceplants: when the thing you're detecting is rare, the base rate dominates everything.

Concrete version. An anomaly detector catches 99% of true anomalies and false-alarms on only 1% of normal readings—a genuinely excellent detector. Anomalies occur in 1 of every 10,000 readings. Now push a million readings through it:

  • 100 are true anomalies → it flags 99 of them.
  • 999,900 are normal → it false-alarms on ~10,000 of them.

So of ~10,100 alerts, 99 are real: an alert is right about 1% of the time. Nothing is wrong with the detector; the base rate simply means false positives outnumber true positives a hundred to one. This is Bayes' theorem operating as intuition—no formula needed, just the two counts—and it's the entire reason "accuracy" is a near-useless metric on imbalanced problems and precision and recall exist as separate numbers (defined properly in the Classical ML Vocabulary primer). It's also the correct frame for every "AI-powered detection" vendor pitch: the question is never the headline accuracy, it's the base rate of the thing being detected, which sets the alert-to-noise ratio your team will actually live with.

Phase 4 extends the same probability language inward: a language model's raw output is a probability distribution over tokens (softmax turns scores into probabilities; sampling draws from them), and log-probabilities are how those distributions get compared. Same vocabulary, one level down the stack.
